“The Bliss in Ignorance”: An Analytical Exercise on Alan Shapiro’s Vigil

Rhetoric of Health and Medicine · Dr. Jennifer K. Edwell · UC Berkeley

October 29, 2020

Topic

The bliss in ignorance.

Evidence

  • “I lived, we all lived, with a doubled, dreamlike consciousness of what we all were going through, bewildered most by what remained familiar…” (5)
  • “For years my father would not accept Russ as his son-in-law” (7)
  • “We were tired of our helplessness, and guilty for being tired. That we were all impatient to go home was our unspoken with, our dirty secret” (10)
  • “Beth… stopped asking for food or liquid, and my mother stopped offering, even though she knew not getting her to eat or drink would hasten her death” (15)
  • “To touch her now, I felt, would be to touch the brute, unvarnished fact of her dying; it would be to face her” (25)

Analysis

The common saying that ignorance is bliss is only a cliche because it is evidently true. That idea is extended as a theme throughout Alan Shapiro’s Vigil, as he explains what got him through the weeks leading to his sister’s slow and agonizing death. When he first visited his sister after her cancer spread into her brain, he didn’t know how to react. He reminisces about how their relationship was always carried on conversation and their cleverness and wit. Seeing her so broken made him feel uncomfortable, describing her vulnerability with words like “brute” and “unvarnished”, he seemed repulsed by her (25). To touch her was to make it real, it would physically confirm that his sister was being consumed by death. Instead of facing that truth, he took the easier route of ignoring the signs, and let himself wallow in his own ignorance.

It wasn’t just Alan that chose to bless himself with ignorance, everyone in the family did too. At the beginning of the book, he explains that while his family was gathered at the hospice, it was easier to go through the motions of everyday life rather than acknowledge the reason why they were gathered there. Alan admits he and his father would conversate about the most normal of things, as if “nothing in the world were more important” (4). Even Beth’s husband would choose to play ignorant in the days preparing for her death. Instead of spending every last minute mourning with Beth, Alan and Russ went to prepare for her cremation. Alan recalls the memory with contentment, “seeking refuge” in the tedious tasks to keep his mind away from reality.

Interpretation

Given this evidence, I contend that Shapiro’s relationship with Beth in her final days represents his wish to ignore death.
